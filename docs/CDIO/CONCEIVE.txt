1. PURPOSE
----------

We are building a desk-scale scanning sonar for a teaching lab. An ultrasonic
sensor on a servo sweeps a 150 degree arc, measures the distance to whatever is
in front of it, and draws each echo on a radar-style screen at the angle the
sensor was pointing.

The one thing it must get right is honesty. A student should be able to trust
where a point is drawn, and the display must make clear what it does NOT know:
how old each point is, when nothing echoed back, and when the sensor has failed.
A radar-looking screen invites people to trust it more than an ultrasonic sensor
deserves, so the display should show the sensor's limits rather than hide them.

Three modes. How the user switches between them (for example by pressing the
joystick) is still open: it is a design decision in D2.
  SWEEP  - automatic back-and-forth scan (the core product)
  LOCK   - scans until it confirms an object, then sweeps a narrow sector
           around it and follows it, with a full re-scan every few seconds
  MANUAL - the joystick points the sensor like a torch, with a live distance

Alternative framing we considered: obstacle awareness for a small robot
or a blind spot. We chose the teaching-lab framing because the brief rewards
evidence and explained limits, and because ultrasonic sensing is not reliable
enough for safety-critical obstacle detection (see section 7).

Previous student projects (from the brief's resources):

  ESP32 + HC-SR04 + SG90, swept 30 to 120 degrees in 1 degree steps, drawn on a
  phone web page. Useful lessons:

  data. [CHECK] still to read in detail (PLAN task C3).

and limits, and an honest display (age + three statuses).

  prototype the display on simulated data and decide once we know the screen.

SC6  | Honest display: echo / no echo / failed told apart without     | T-FAULT,
     | colour; points older than one pass visibly faded; STALE warning| T-STALE
     | within 1.5 s if readings stop                                  |
SC7  | Latency: a reading is on screen within 200 ms of the ping      | T-LAT
SC8  | LOCK (extension): locks onto a single tube within 2 passes;    | T-LOCK
     | keeps the lock while it is moved by hand at about 10 cm/s at   |
     | 50 cm; back to full sweep within 2 s after it is removed; no   |
     | false lock in an empty scene over 2 minutes                    |
SC9  | MANUAL (extension): sensor follows the stick within 0.3 s and  | T-JOY
     | does not creep when the stick is let go                        |
SC10 | Usability: someone outside the team, with no explanation,      | O2 user
     | places 2 objects within +/-10 cm and +/-10 degrees and can say | test
     | which points are old                                           |

Why these numbers: the HC-SR04 datasheet gives a 2 to 400 cm range and about
a 15 degree beam. We chose 150 cm because the system is desk-scale, and
because accuracy falls off with range: the echo gets weaker, so thin or soft
objects drop out, and the beam gets wider (about 39 cm across at 150 cm), so
objects are harder to place. Range does not make the scan faster: our timing
model (PLAN task S1) showed that at a 60 ms ping gap the pass time is the
same at 100, 150 or 200 cm, because the gap sets the pace, not the echo
timeout. +/-5 degrees is a third of the beam width, and needs the
edge-midpoint method rather than the nearest bin. 5 s per pass: the timing
model gives 4.5 s for one 150 degree pass at a 2 degree step and the 60 ms
ping gap the datasheet recommends (docs/evidence/sim/s1_summary.txt).

7. IS ULTRASONIC SENSING APPROPRIATE?
-------------------------------------

For a desk-scale teaching demo: yes. For anything safety-critical: no.

Good for:
  - Cheap, safe, simple to time with a microcontroller (about 58 us per cm of
    distance, round trip).
  - Works in the dark, and sees glass and clear plastic, which light-based
    sensors can miss.

Limitations we expect, which we will demonstrate and measure:
  - Wide beam (about 15 degrees). At 1 m it is about 26 cm wide, so a thin
    object is smeared across several angles, and two objects close together
    merge into one.
  - Specular reflection: a smooth surface at an angle bounces the sound away,
    so a wall or box can be invisible when angled.
  - Soft materials (cloth, foam, clothing) absorb sound and may not echo.
  - Slow: about 60 ms per ping, so a full scan takes seconds. Fast-moving
    objects are in the wrong place by the time the sweep comes back.
  - SWEEP mode cannot follow a walking person. The sweep goes back and
    forth, so a point is measured again after 1 pass (middle of the arc) to
    2 passes (ends), 4.5 to 9 s. Someone walking at 0.2 m/s has moved 0.9 to
    1.8 m by then, several times the beam width (about 26 cm at 1 m). Our
    timing model (PLAN task S1) gives these numbers. This is one reason for
    LOCK mode: sweeping only a narrow sector around a target revisits it
    much more often.
  - Objects at the ends of the arc (15 and 165 degrees): only half the object
    is seen, so its bearing is pulled about 4.5 degrees inward. The display
    marks these bearings as low confidence (S2).
    Evidence: docs/evidence/sim/s2_summary.txt.
  - Two thin objects closer than about 17 degrees apart show as one (S2;
    T-TWO checks). Evidence: docs/evidence/sim/s2_summary.txt.
  - A flat board tilted more than about 35 to 40 degrees from the sensor gives
    no echo, so "no echo" does not mean "clear" (S2; T-MAT checks).
    Evidence: docs/evidence/sim/s2_summary.txt.
  - The datasheet's accuracy (up to 3 mm) assumes a target of at least
    0.5 m^2 that is flat and smooth. Our SC1 target (a 5 cm tube) is far
    smaller than that, so the datasheet figures don't cover it. Our own
    T-DIST and T-MAT measurements are the evidence for SC1, not the datasheet.
  - Every HC-SR04 uses 40 kHz, so a nearby team's sensor can cause false echoes.
  - Speed of sound changes with temperature (about 0.17 % per degree C), which
    shifts distances slightly.
  - 40 kHz is above human hearing but within the hearing range of cats and
    some dogs.

Consequences of a missed or misleading detection:
  - Missed object (soft, thin or angled): the user thinks the space is empty.
    In a teaching lab that means a wrong conclusion; in an obstacle-avoidance
    product it would be a collision. So we never claim "clear": the display
    shows "no echo", not "nothing there".
  - Old point shown as current: the user thinks an object is still there,
    or misses that it moved. So points fade, and a STALE warning appears.
  - Wrong angle (beam smear, servo not settled): an object is drawn where it
    is not. So we plan to tag readings with the settled angle and try an
    edge-midpoint method in LOCK, and measure angle accuracy (T-ANG).
  - Crosstalk or a late echo: a phantom object appears. So pings are spaced
    at least 60 ms apart, and we test with a neighbour's scanner on (T-XTALK).


8. SONAR VS RADAR
-----------------

Our display looks like a radar screen, but the system is a sonar: it uses
sound, not radio waves.

                     | Our sonar (HC-SR04)          | Radar
---------------------|------------------------------|------------------------------
Wave                 | Sound (pressure wave in air) | Radio (electromagnetic)
Speed                | ~343 m/s                     | ~300,000,000 m/s
Time for 1 cm        | ~58 us round trip            | ~67 ps round trip
Needs a medium?      | Yes (air, water)             | No (works in a vacuum)
Beam                 | ~15 deg, fixed by transducer | Set by the antenna; can be
                     |                              | steered electronically
Scanning here        | Servo turns the sensor       | Rotating dish or phased array
Weather / air        | Affected by wind, temperature| Works through fog and rain
Speed of target      | Not measured (we only have   | Measured directly from the
                     | distance)                    | Doppler shift
Range                | Metres in air                | Up to hundreds of km

What they have in common: both send a pulse, time the echo, and calculate distance =
speed x time / 2. Both show results on a sweep display.

What would need to change beyond the display to make this a radar: a radio
transmitter and receiver, an antenna, and timing electronics about a million
times faster (picoseconds instead of microseconds). A microcontroller timer
can measure sound echoes directly but cannot time radio echoes, so real radars
use special techniques (for example FMCW, which measures a frequency difference
instead of a time).


9. ETHICS, PRIVACY, SAFETY, ACCESSIBILITY, SUSTAINABILITY
---------------------------------------------------------

Privacy and ethics
  - The scanner cannot identify anyone, but it can detect when someone is
    present and moving. Combined with logging or a phone link, that becomes
    monitoring.
  - Our choices: no logging unless the user starts it, data stays on the
    device / USB link, and if the phone stretch is built it runs on a
    password-protected local network. If it were used where people are
    present, they should be told (a sign).
  - Over-trust: a radar-style display looks authoritative. We show its limits
    (age, no-echo, failed) so people don't trust it more than it deserves.

Safety
  - Low voltage (USB 5 V). The moving head is slow and weak, so the pinch
    risk is minimal, but the mount keeps fingers and wires away from the servo
    horn. Animals may hear 40 kHz, so we don't leave it running near pets.

Accessibility
  - Every status has a shape and text as well as a colour. Large distance
    readout. The display does not rely on sound.

Sustainability (Week 8 lecture: energy, materials, e-waste, repairability)
  - Reuses kit parts; nothing is glued, so parts go back in the box.
  - The servo is released (no pulses) when paused, so it stops drawing current
    and buzzing. We will measure current per mode (T-POWER).
  - Mount held with screws and plug-in connectors, so it is repairable.
